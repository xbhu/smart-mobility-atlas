abstract: |-
  Highly automated vehicle (HAV) deployments in the United States are expanding rapidly, yet the policy discourse surrounding them remains shaped by misconceptions about what the technology actually is, who can legally operate it, and what it takes to scale economically, among others. This paper argues that these misconceptions are not information deficits solely resolvable through public education. They are products of three structural forces: (1) American federalism, which distributes regulatory authority across 50 states with no binding federal framework; (2) market incentives, which reward companies for blurring the boundary between driver-assistance and autonomous driving; and (3) legislative lag, which allows statutes to outpace the enforcement infrastructure needed to implement them. Drawing on expert focus groups with 35 participants from 29 organizations, a cross-state regulatory comparison covering all 50 states and the District of Columbia, and our direct involvement in evaluating Pennsylvania's Act 130, the paper poses six questions that expose how these forces produce specific policy failures. Among the findings: no new vehicle a consumer can currently purchase operates beyond SAE Level 2—the consumer "self-driving car" does not exist in any legally meaningful sense; and a single interstate freight trip can cross six states with six incompatible regulatory regimes, insurance requirements ranging from zero to five million dollars, and no automatic interstate reciprocity. The paper concludes that state-level fragmentation is a durable institutional feature of American HAV governance, not a temporary phase, and recommends structural responses: differentiated regulation by automation level, interstate compacts for freight corridor harmonization, mandatory disclosure of operational economics, and standardized enforcement protocols. Autonomous freight is identified as the highest-return near-term priority.

## What problem does this research solve?

Highly automated vehicles (HAVs), meaning vehicles that drive themselves at SAE Level 3 or above, are no longer a research idea. Waymo robotaxis carry paying riders in several U.S. cities, Aurora runs driverless freight trucks between Dallas and Houston, and the Autonomous Vehicle Industry Association reports more than 145 million autonomous miles on U.S. public roads.

Public understanding has not kept up. Most Americans cannot tell a driver-assistance system such as Tesla's "Full Self-Driving (Supervised)" from a Level 4 system that needs no driver. Many assume they can buy an HAV and drive it home, although in many states only companies and institutions may hold an operating certificate. "Driverless" services still depend on remote human operators. And a single federal rulebook has been "coming soon" for a decade.

These misunderstandings have real costs. Argo AI, a Pittsburgh self-driving company once valued at $12.4 billion, dissolved on the same day Pennsylvania signed Act 130, its first comprehensive HAV law. Its successor, StackAV, stayed in the state largely because Act 130 finally gave it a legal path to test on public roads.

The paper argues that better public education alone will not close this gap, because three structural forces keep reproducing it:

- **American federalism.** States, not Washington, decide who may operate what vehicle and under what conditions. The result is 50 separate rulebooks with no binding federal framework.
- **Market incentives.** Companies gain sales, subscriptions and investor interest by blurring the line between driver assistance and genuine autonomy.
- **Legislative lag.** Legislatures can pass an HAV law in one session; the police training, emergency protocols and reporting systems needed to enforce it take years.

## What did we do?

We partnered with the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation (PennDOT) to evaluate the impact of Act 130 of 2022. As part of that evaluation, we ran expert focus groups in September 2024: five group sessions and three individual sessions with 35 participants from 29 organizations, from self-driving companies and trade groups to state agencies, transit operators and cities.

The focus groups were part of a policy evaluation, not a formal qualitative study, so the paper treats them as case-based evidence of how the law works in practice, mostly from Pennsylvania. Two more evidence streams carry the national picture: a review of HAV statutes in all 50 states and the District of Columbia, with federal legislative history since 2017, and publicly available industry data on costs and safety, along with national consumer surveys.

The six questions that structure the paper were not set in advance. Each one marks a place where these three evidence streams pointed to the same policy failure.

## What are the key findings?

### 1. Is your car really "self-driving"? No car you can buy today is.

As of March 2026, no new vehicle a consumer can buy operates beyond SAE Level 2. At Level 2 the driver must watch the road at all times and is legally responsible for the car, however capable the system seems. At Level 3 and above, the system does the driving within set limits and the manufacturer, not the driver, takes responsibility. That line decides who is liable in a crash and whether HAV law applies at all.

The marketing tells a different story. In a AAA Foundation survey, only 43% of U.S. drivers could correctly define a fully automated vehicle, and nearly one in ten believed they could already buy a car that drives itself while they sleep. In December 2025 the California DMV ruled that Tesla's "Autopilot" and "Full Self-Driving" branding violated state law; Tesla sued the DMV rather than change the names.

### 2. Who is allowed to put an HAV on the road? It depends entirely on the state.

Of the 34 states with some legal framework for HAVs, only 11 have detailed, AV-specific rules. Pennsylvania grants certificates only to organizations. Texas requires authorization for commercial operators but exempts individuals driving for personal use. Florida requires no permit at all. New York allows testing only under state police supervision. As the paper puts it, a Pennsylvanian who bought a Level 4 vehicle could not legally operate it at home; a Texan could.

### 3. Should all levels of automation be regulated the same way? Most laws do, and they should not.

Most state laws, including Act 130, put Levels 3, 4 and 5 under one "HAV" definition. But a Level 3 car with a licensed driver at the wheel and a Level 4 truck with nobody in the cab pose very different risks. Several company and trade association representatives in the focus groups recommended that Level 3 should not need a PennDOT certificate at all. The European Union already approves Level 3 systems separately under UNECE Regulation 157, which shows that differentiated rules are workable.

### 4. What does it take for "driverless" vehicles to scale? Fewer humans behind each vehicle.

"Driverless" does not mean "humanless." Every HAV on public roads today is backed by remote operators who monitor it and step in at tricky moments. How many vehicles one operator can handle may be the single most important cost factor unique to driverless operation.

Long-haul freight is the most likely to close that gap first: highways have no pedestrians or traffic signals and far fewer unusual situations. A human-driven truck is limited by hours-of-service rules to roughly 100,000 to 125,000 miles a year; an autonomous truck can run 250,000 or more. Aurora launched the first U.S. commercial driverless freight service in May 2025 and reported more than 250,000 driverless miles with no at-fault collisions. Still, working technology does not guarantee a working business: TuSimple, Embark and Argo AI all left the field.

### 5. Can an HAV company operate across state lines? Not under one set of rules.

The freight run at the top of this page shows the problem. Between Pennsylvania and Texas, the truck passes from a state that requires a licensed driver, to one that allows driverless operation, to one with no AV law at all, then into Tennessee, one of the most permissive states, and on through Arkansas and Texas, each with conditions of its own. Insurance rules range from zero to five million dollars, and no state has to recognize another's certificate: a vehicle certified in California cannot operate in Pennsylvania without an interstate agreement approved by the Governor.

That leaves companies with two poor options. They can follow the strictest rule everywhere, for example keeping a safety driver even where none is required, and give up the savings of driverless operation. Or they can run a different operating model in each state and multiply their compliance costs. Help from Washington is unlikely.

### 6. What happens after the law is signed? Enforcement lags for years.

How does a police officer pull over a car with no driver? No national protocol exists. In 2023 a Cruise robotaxi in San Francisco pulled away from officers during a traffic stop, an incident that contributed to the suspension of its permit. Firefighters and paramedics often lack training to shut down an automated vehicle after a crash while also handling its high-voltage battery. Act 130 requires operators to notify municipalities ten days before starting service, but many Pennsylvania towns have no designated contact to receive that notice. Pennsylvania signed Act 130 in November 2022; its certificate guidelines came out in October 2024, nearly two years later.

### Why these gaps will not close on their own

The three forces feed each other. Under federalism, each state faces aggressive marketing and slow enforcement alone. Marketing claims thrive because no single state regulator can police a national campaign. And each state must build its own enforcement capacity from scratch.

A brief look abroad shows how distinctly American this is. The EU has binding Level 3 rules and is extending them to Level 4. The United Kingdom's Automated Vehicles Act 2024 created a national authorization scheme and separates true self-driving from driver assistance by law. China runs city pilot zones under national guidelines, and Japan amended its Road Traffic Act in 2023 to permit Level 4 driving under a permit system. Fifty independent rulebooks with no binding national framework is a U.S. condition.

## What can this be used for?

The paper turns its diagnosis into six recommendations, each aimed at one of the three forces:

1. **Standard consumer labels for automation levels**, like a fuel-economy sticker, to counter marketing that rewards ambiguity.
2. **Separate rules for Level 3 and for Level 4/5**, following the EU's approach, to match oversight to actual risk.
3. **Interstate compacts or mutual recognition agreements**, starting with freight corridors in the eastern states, instead of waiting for federal preemption.
4. **Standard police and emergency-response protocols as a condition of certification**, with funded first-responder training and named municipal contacts.
5. **Public disclosure of remote-operator ratios and operating economics**, so "driverless" claims can be checked.
6. **Harmonized rules for autonomous freight corridors as the top near-term priority**, because freight is the use most likely to pay off and the one most limited by state borders.

Who can use this work:

- **State DOTs and legislators**: a structural explanation of why HAV laws underperform after they pass, with recommendations matched to each cause.
- **Companies planning interstate operations**: a concrete picture of what one Pennsylvania-to-Texas freight run requires today.
- **Researchers in AV governance**: a framework of federalism, market incentives and legislative lag that also applies to other emerging technologies regulated state by state.
